# Who Gets The Bus? — Toronto Transit Equity Map

An interactive map of all **158 City of Toronto neighbourhoods** that shows, for
each one, the gap between **how much the neighbourhood depends on transit** and
**how well transit actually serves it**.

The map has three switchable layers:

- **Equity gap** — the headline layer: transit *need* minus transit *access*.
- **Access** — the transit-access score on its own.
- **Need** — the transit-need score on its own.

Hover any neighbourhood for its scores and the underlying numbers (stop count,
peak-hour service, low-income share, car-free commuting, population density). An
**"About this map"** panel in the page explains the method in plain language, and
a **"Download data (.xlsx)"** button exports every neighbourhood's scores as a
spreadsheet.

## What "transit equity gap" means

Some neighbourhoods lean on transit far more than others — because incomes are
lower, because fewer households have a car, because people live densely enough
that driving everywhere doesn't scale. Call that **need**. Separately, transit
serves some neighbourhoods well and others poorly — measured here by how many
stops sit in the neighbourhood and how often vehicles come through in the weekday
morning rush. Call that **access**. The **equity gap** is simply *need minus
access*. A large positive gap (shown in pink) is a neighbourhood that depends on
transit but doesn't get much of it. A negative gap (blue) means service runs
ahead of need. It is a screening tool for "where is the mismatch worst", not a
verdict on any single route.

## A snapshot, not a live feed

This is a **single point-in-time snapshot**. It was built from City of Toronto
open data **retrieved 1 September 2026**, using a TTC schedule feed valid
**6 September – 31 October 2026** and **2021 Census** neighbourhood profiles. It
is **not** updated as routes, schedules, demographics, or neighbourhood
boundaries change. Re-running the pipeline (below) against fresher data would
produce a new snapshot.

## How to reproduce the pipeline

All computation happens **offline, once**. There is no server, database, or
scheduled job — the deliverable is a folder of static files.

### Setup

```bash
python -m venv venv
venv/Scripts/activate        # Windows;  source venv/bin/activate on macOS/Linux
pip install -r requirements.txt
```

### Run the scripts in order

Each script prints a validation/summary report and writes its output file. Data
flows **`data/raw/` → `data/processed/` → `output/`**.

| # | Script | What it does | Writes |
|---|--------|--------------|--------|
| 00 | `scripts/00_fetch_data.py` | Discovers and downloads the three source datasets from the City of Toronto open-data API. Run once with `--download`. | `data/raw/` (GTFS zip, neighbourhood polygons, 2021 profile spreadsheet) |
| 01 | `scripts/01_validate.py` | Checks the GTFS service calendar and confirms the boundary ↔ profile join key still matches 1:1. Fails loudly if an assumption broke. | console only |
| 02 | `scripts/02_stop_frequency.py` | Filters the schedule to a normal weekday 7–9 am and counts trips per stop — both a plain count and one weighted by vehicle capacity (bus/streetcar/LRT/subway). | `data/processed/stop_frequency.parquet` |
| 03 | `scripts/03_access_score.py` | Assigns stops to neighbourhoods (spatial join), adds a walkshed credit for rapid-transit stations just outside a boundary, and computes **access_score**. | `data/processed/access.parquet` |
| 04 | `scripts/04_need_score.py` | Pulls low-income share, car-free commuting share, and population density from the 2021 profiles and computes **need_score**. | `data/processed/need.parquet` |
| 05 | `scripts/05_equity_gap.py` | Merges access + need on the neighbourhood key and computes **equity_gap = need − access**. | `data/processed/equity.parquet`, `data/processed/equity.geojson` |
| 06 | `scripts/06_build_map.py` | Renders the Leaflet map, the three layers, the dark UI shell, the About panel, and the spreadsheet-export button into one self-contained file. | `output/index.html` |

```bash
venv/Scripts/python scripts/00_fetch_data.py --download
venv/Scripts/python scripts/01_validate.py
venv/Scripts/python scripts/02_stop_frequency.py
venv/Scripts/python scripts/03_access_score.py
venv/Scripts/python scripts/04_need_score.py
venv/Scripts/python scripts/05_equity_gap.py
venv/Scripts/python scripts/06_build_map.py
```

`data/raw/` is left untouched after download; all cleaning writes to
`data/processed/`. `data/processed/` and `output/` are gitignored — they are
rebuilt from the scripts.

## Methodology summary

Every score is computed across all 158 neighbourhoods and each input is scaled to
a 0–1 range before being combined, so the pieces are comparable.

**Need score** (0 = least transit-dependent, 1 = most) — the average of three
equally weighted, city-ranked inputs:

- **Low income** — the share of residents below the Low-income Measure, after tax
  (LIM-AT).
- **Car-free commuting** — the share of commuters who get to work by something
  other than a car (walking, cycling, transit).
- **Population density** — people per square kilometre.

**Access score** (0 = worst served, 1 = best) — a weighted blend of:

- **Stop density** (weight 0.6) — TTC stops in the neighbourhood per square
  kilometre.
- **Peak service** (weight 0.4) — scheduled trips through those stops on a
  weekday between 7 and 9 am, **weighted by vehicle capacity** (roughly bus ×1,
  streetcar ×2.5, LRT ×6, subway ×15, so a subway train doesn't count the same
  as one bus), **plus** partial credit for busy subway/LRT stations within 500 m
  of the neighbourhood's edge (full credit at the boundary, fading to zero at
  500 m ≈ a six-minute walk).

**Equity gap** = need − access. In this snapshot it ranges from about **−0.29**
(Annex — service well ahead of need) to about **+0.46** (North St. James Town —
high need, service not keeping pace). Other neighbourhoods near the underserved
end include North Toronto, Harbourfront–CityPlace, Yonge–Doris, and South
Parkdale.

The exact formulas, weights, capacity figures, and every judgment call are
documented in [`METHODOLOGY.md`](METHODOLOGY.md).

## Known limitations

This is an approximate, transparent model — useful for spotting mismatches, not
for adjudicating them. The main caveats, in short (the full treatment is in
[`METHODOLOGY.md`](METHODOLOGY.md) §3):

- **Bloor-Yonge counts as two stations.** The TTC schedule feed names the two
  halves of the Bloor-Yonge interchange separately ("Bloor Station" and "Yonge
  Station", ~100 m apart) with no link to join them, so the pipeline does not
  merge them. Neighbourhoods around that interchange may show slightly more
  walkshed credit than they strictly should.
- **The demographic inputs are stand-ins.** The 2021 Census does not publish a
  "low-income households" figure or a household vehicle-ownership question, so
  LIM-AT prevalence and non-car commute share are used as the closest available
  proxies. The direction is right; the exact definitions differ from the
  original intent. See [`METHODOLOGY.md`](METHODOLOGY.md) §2c for the reasoning.
- **Density can't distinguish "dense and low-income" from "dense and affluent
  car-free".** Population density is one of three equally weighted need inputs
  and rewards any dense neighbourhood, so a downtown condo district where people
  are car-free by choice can score high need even though its residents aren't
  transit-dependent in the way the map intends. Read a high need score alongside
  its low-income component.
- **Several parameters are round-number judgment calls** — the 500 m walkshed
  radius, linear (not exponential) distance decay, the capacity multipliers, the
  0.4 / 0.6 access weighting, the 7–9 am window. They are defensible and
  deliberately simple, not tuned or measured; none is derived from a single
  objectively correct method.
- **It reflects one moment.** 2021 Census demographics and a specific
  autumn-2026 TTC schedule feed — not current conditions, and not an average
  over time.
- **West Humber-Clairville is a genuine outlier.** By far the largest
  neighbourhood by area (mostly Pearson airport and industrial land, few
  residents); because normalization is anchored on the extremes, it compresses
  the access scale for everyone else. Left in as-is — dropping or clipping it
  would be its own arbitrary choice.

## Data sources and licence

All three datasets come from the **City of Toronto Open Data portal**
([open.toronto.ca](https://open.toronto.ca)) and are licensed under the
**[Open Government Licence – Toronto](https://open.toronto.ca/open-data-license/)**.

| Dataset | Used for |
|---------|----------|
| [TTC Routes and Schedules (GTFS)](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/ttc-routes-and-schedules/) | stops, trips, schedules → access score |
| [Neighbourhoods](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/neighbourhoods/) | the 158 neighbourhood boundaries → areas, map geometry |
| [Neighbourhood Profiles](https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/neighbourhood-profiles/) | 2021 Census income, commuting, population → need score |

The pipeline discovers the right file in each dataset at download time rather
than hard-coding filenames. The exact CKAN package IDs and the specific resources
used are listed in [`METHODOLOGY.md`](METHODOLOGY.md) §4.

## Tech stack

The offline pipeline is **Python**: `pandas` / `numpy` for the tabular scoring
math, `geopandas` / `shapely` for the neighbourhood polygons and the
stops-to-neighbourhoods spatial join, and `partridge` for reading a filtered
slice of the large GTFS schedule feed. The map is built with `folium`
(Leaflet.js) plus `branca` for the colour scales, and rendered to one
self-contained HTML file. The in-page spreadsheet export uses
[SheetJS](https://sheetjs.com/), vendored into the page so it runs entirely in
the browser. Full dependency list in [`requirements.txt`](requirements.txt).
